Where the period range comes from

Published averagesYour own pattern
0 cycles ▲ you: 2 12

Sanguine looks at the lengths of your last 12 cycles. Before you have logged a few, it starts from published averages for your age group (Bull et al. 2019)1 and moves towards your own pattern as cycles are added. In most life states, recent cycles and older ones count the same. A cycle that looks like a missed log, or that falls in a life change you set (such as pregnancy, or the months after stopping a hormonal method), is left out or counted less. The result is the range of days that has an 80% likelihood of holding your next period start; the calendar also shows the wider 95% range.

2

What the confidence words mean

Confident: at least six cycles and a period range no wider than about a week.
Fair estimate: at least two cycles and a period range of up to about 11 days.
Rough estimate: fewer than two cycles, or cycles that vary so much the range is wider than about 11 days. The range is wide on purpose.
No estimate: not enough data yet, or a life change you set pauses the estimates.
3

What narrows the range

Every period start Morning temperature Ovulation tests Cervical mucus Cervical position

Logging every period start, including the first day, matters most. Morning temperature, ovulation tests, cervical mucus and cervical position can date ovulation in a cycle after it happens, which sharpens the next period estimate and the fertile window estimate.2

4

What does not change the estimates

Symptoms Mood Notes
Insights Estimates

Symptoms, mood and notes never change an estimate. They show up in Insights so you can see your own patterns, but there is no published evidence that they predict when a period starts.

How well it has done for you

Drawn to explain, not your own data.

When a cycle ends, Sanguine checks whether the range it gave at the start of that cycle held the real start date. Insights, Your accuracy, shows the count.
